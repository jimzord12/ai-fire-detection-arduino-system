# 5.2 Power Management and Thermal Considerations

Power management in multi-sensor edge nodes is inseparable from the thermal characteristics of the gas-sensing components embedded within the system. In the context of this fire detection node, the dominant power consumers are not the microcontrollers or the wireless subsystem in steady-state operation, but rather the resistive heating elements integrated into each Metal Oxide Semiconductor (MOS) gas sensor. Understanding the electrical and thermal behavior of these elements is therefore a prerequisite for designing a node capable of sustained, battery-tolerant operation at the edge.

## 5.2.1 Active Heating Elements in MOS Gas Sensors

Metal oxide gas sensors—including those used for smoke, VOC, and CO detection in this project—rely on a fundamental operating principle: the chemiresistive reaction between oxygen adsorbates in the metal oxide layer and target gas molecules is strongly temperature-dependent (Chen et al., 2021). To drive this reaction at a sufficient rate, the sensing material must be maintained at an elevated operating temperature, typically between 200 °C and 400 °C (Chen et al., 2021). This thermal activation is achieved by a microheater element integrated directly beneath or around the sensing film, which converts electrical power into Joule heat to sustain the required surface temperature.

In conventional ceramic-substrate or wire-wound gas sensors, this heating requirement imposes a severe power penalty. Commercially available non-MEMS sensors such as the Figaro TGS2620 and the Nissha FIS SP series consume between 210 mW and 400 mW per sensor at their rated operating temperatures (Chen et al., 2021). For a multi-sensor node carrying three such gas sensors (smoke, VOC, CO), the aggregate heater load would exceed 600 mW—an untenable budget for battery-powered edge deployment. The advent of MEMS microheater technology fundamentally changes this equation. By suspending the heating element on a thin silicon nitride membrane over an air gap, thermal conduction to the substrate is suppressed, confining heat to the active sensing area (Chen et al., 2021). This suspended architecture reduces heater power consumption to approximately 39 mW at 400 °C for a single sensor, representing an order-of-magnitude improvement over ceramic-substrate equivalents (Chen et al., 2021).

The Fermion MEMS sensors selected for this node operate on this micro-hotplate principle. Their integrated MEMS microheaters not only reduce the absolute power draw but also shorten the thermal time constant of the heating element. Whereas traditional MOX sensors require warm-up periods of 60 to 90 minutes before reaching a stable operating point in static (DC) mode, MEMS micro-hotplates achieve thermal equilibrium within seconds due to their low thermal mass (Xing et al., 2019). This has a direct implication for firmware design: the node can be duty-cycled—powering down the heater supply between measurement windows—rather than maintaining sensors at temperature indefinitely. Such a pulsed heating strategy has been experimentally validated to reduce average power consumption by at least 20% while preserving gas detection sensitivity, as demonstrated by Xing et al. (2019) in a mobile robot gas sensing unit employing thermal modulation between 225 °C and 325 °C.

The pulsed or duty-cycled heating mode also confers a secondary benefit relevant to false alarm discrimination. When the microheater is toggled between two temperatures ("ON" and "OFF" states), the differential response of the metal oxide film encodes kinetic information about the target gas species—specifically, its chemical reaction rate at each temperature (Xing et al., 2019). This dynamic response signature is distinct for different gas types, enabling a low-cost sensor array to perform rudimentary chemical discrimination without increasing sensor count. In the present system, this effect is exploited indirectly: the temporal evolution of sensor readings during the warm-up phase is captured as part of the time-series window fed to the TinyML classifier, providing the model with richer discriminative features than a single steady-state reading.

From a power budgeting perspective, the three MEMS gas sensors (smoke, VOC, CO) collectively draw approximately 60 mW during active heating phases, based on the per-sensor figures reported by Chen et al. (2021). The AHT20 temperature/humidity sensor and the IR flame detector operate passively without heaters, contributing negligible thermal dissipation. The Renesas RA4M1 microcontroller, operating at 48 MHz with the inference engine active, adds approximately 20–30 mW, while the ESP32-S3 WiFi module contributes a burst load of 240 mW during MQTT transmission but remains in deep sleep between telemetry cycles. Therefore, the MEMS sensor heating elements constitute the dominant sustained power load in the node's thermal and electrical budget and must be treated as the primary target of any power optimization strategy.

An additional thermal consideration concerns the influence of heater self-heating on co-located sensors. The AHT20 temperature and humidity sensor, which provides ambient thermal context for the classifier, must be physically isolated from the gas sensor heating elements to avoid artificially elevated temperature readings. Thermal cross-contamination between the MEMS gas sensors and the AHT20 would corrupt the temperature feature channel, introducing systematic bias into the classifier's input. The physical enclosure design (addressed in Section 5.3) must therefore enforce spatial separation between the heater-bearing sensors and the AHT20, with the latter positioned in a thermally decoupled location exposed to ambient airflow rather than the local thermal plume of the gas sensor array.

## Glossary

- **MOS (Metal Oxide Semiconductor) gas sensor**: A chemiresistive sensor whose electrical resistance changes upon exposure to target gases due to surface reactions at an elevated operating temperature.
- **Micro-hotplate (MHP)**: A MEMS-fabricated suspended membrane structure that provides thermal isolation for an integrated heater, dramatically reducing the power required to maintain the sensing film at its operating temperature.
- **Pulsed heating mode**: An operating strategy in which the sensor heater is alternately powered and de-energized, reducing average power consumption while enabling dynamic signal features to be extracted from the sensor's transient thermal response.
- **Thermal cross-contamination**: Unintended heat transfer from one component (e.g., a MEMS gas sensor heater) to an adjacent sensor (e.g., a temperature/humidity sensor), causing measurement errors through local thermal bias.
- **Duty cycling**: The practice of periodically powering down a subsystem between active measurement intervals to reduce average energy consumption over time.
