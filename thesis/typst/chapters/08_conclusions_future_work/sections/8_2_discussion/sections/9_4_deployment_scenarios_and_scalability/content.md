## 9.4.1 Cost Analysis per Node

The bill of materials (BOM) for a single Autonomous Sensing Node is determined by the Arduino UNO R4 WiFi platform and its five DFRobot MEMS sensor peripherals. The Arduino UNO R4 WiFi (Renesas RA4M1 + ESP32-S3 co-processor) retails at approximately USD 27.50. The five sensors — smoke (SEN0570), IR flame (DFR0076), VOC (SEN0566), CO (SEN0564), and temperature/humidity (SEN0527) — contribute an estimated additional USD 40–60 in MEMS module cost at single-unit pricing. Passive prototyping components (breadboard, jumper wires, resistors) add negligible cost, yielding a per-node BOM in the range of USD 70–90 at prototype quantity. This figure compares favourably with commercial four-wire addressable fire detector points, which typically carry a list price of USD 80–200 exclusive of installation labour, panel, and wiring infrastructure. ## 9.4.2 Mesh Network Topology for Multi-Node Systems

A single Autonomous Sensing Node operating in isolation provides point-coverage fire detection; scalable building-wide protection requires multiple nodes to be integrated into a coherent monitoring fabric. The ESP32-S3 radio co-processor of the UNO R4 WiFi natively supports both WiFi 4 (IEEE 802.11b/g/n, 2.4 GHz) and Bluetooth 5 LE, enabling two complementary topologies: a star topology in which each Edge Intelligence Unit publishes MQTT telemetry directly to a local broker (e.g., a Raspberry Pi or cloud endpoint), and a hybrid mesh topology using ESP-NOW as the intra-node transport with a designated gateway node forwarding aggregated payloads over MQTT. The latter pattern decouples sensing from connectivity: sensor nodes transmit compact, low-latency ESP-NOW frames while only the gateway node maintains a persistent WiFi association, substantially reducing channel contention in dense deployments. [ppl-ai-file-upload.s3.amazonaws](https://ppl-ai-file-upload.s3.amazonaws.com/web/direct-files/collection_3f9a5a2d-65e4-4fce-84eb-a32f30c9bde8/d1377c84-3ed3-4d0d-b9b5-8736461c26bc/THESIS_OUTLINE.md)

Location awareness across a multi-node installation is implemented as _Static Location Tagging_: each node's MQTT payload carries a hardcoded `Zone_ID` field (e.g., `Zone_ID: Kitchen_North`, `Zone_ID: Server_Room_East`), providing room-level spatial resolution without GPS hardware. This approach is well suited to the target application of stationary autonomous nodes for building automation, home safety, and industrial monitoring, where physical positions are fixed at installation time. Prior work on fault-tolerant IoT fire detection architectures has demonstrated that an ad-hoc wireless network of multi-sensor nodes — equipped with smoke, humidity, temperature, CO, and methane sensors — can be deployed throughout a building with each node acting as an independent sensing and relay unit, improving system resilience by eliminating single points of failure. Regarding protocol scalability, LoRaWAN-based studies have shown that a single gateway can sustain up to 6 000 end nodes when 75% of nodes transmit once per hour; while the present architecture targets WiFi-based MQTT, this benchmark illustrates the practical upper bound achievable when a lightweight message protocol is paired with appropriate radio technology. The Asymmetric Multi-Processing architecture of the UNO R4 WiFi is directly applicable to multi-node mesh deployment: the Renesas RA4M1 "Brain" executes TinyML inference and manages sensor acquisition at 10 Hz — generating a classification result every ≤ 100 ms — while the ESP32-S3 "Radio" handles all wireless telemetry asynchronously. This separation ensures that transient WiFi congestion or broker unavailability does not interrupt local inference, preserving the reflexive detection capability of the node even during network partitions — a critical property for life-safety applications.

For building-scale deployments, a hierarchical three-tier architecture is recommended: sensor nodes perform local inference and threshold-based alerting at the edge; an edge gateway aggregates MQTT streams from all nodes for zone-level correlation and dashboard visualisation; and an optional cloud tier provides long-term logging and remote configuration. Future extensions of the system could incorporate fault-tolerant sensor fusion, enabling continued fire detection via the remaining chemical and thermal channels should the IR flame sensor be blocked or malfunction — a design objective explicitly identified in the system's development roadmap. ## 9.4.3 Certification Challenges (UL/CE)

The pathway from a functional research prototype to a commercially deployable fire safety product is governed by stringent regulatory frameworks on both sides of the Atlantic. In the European market, fire detection and alarm system (FDAS) components must comply with the EN 54 series of standards; EN 54-29 and EN 54-31 specifically address multi-sensor fire detectors — directly applicable to a sensor-fusion node — and mandate that testing, inspection, and certification be conducted by a Notified Body under the EU Construction Products Regulation (EU 305/2011). The CE marking required for EU market access is not self-declarable for FDAS products; it follows mandatory third-party conformity assessment, meaning that a Notified Body must verify that the device meets the relevant EN 54 detection performance, environmental immunity, and electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) requirements. For compatibility and connectability assessment between FDAS components — including transmission paths over radio-frequency links — EN 54-13 applies, specifying requirements for subsystem integration at field, control, and management levels. [sirsyeduniversity.edu](https://sirsyeduniversity.edu.pk/ssurj/rj/index.php/ssurj/article/download/410/172/2023)

In the North American market, equivalent obligations are imposed by UL standards: UL 268 governs smoke detectors for fire alarm signalling systems, UL 521 covers heat detectors, and UL 864 applies to fire alarm control units. Products certified under EN 54 by a Notified Body (such as VdS) that also carry the CE marking can, under NFPA 72, be used in installations designed to that standard, providing a degree of mutual recognition. However, UL listing requires separate factory audits and performance testing tailored to North American installation and environmental conditions. [ijsred](https://ijsred.com/volume8/issue5/IJSRED-V8I5P301.pdf)

<localData>
The Autonomous Sensing Node as currently prototyped is explicitly a research-grade edge intelligence platform intended to demonstrate the feasibility of TinyML-based three-class fire classification on commodity embedded hardware. Its BOM — Arduino UNO R4 WiFi on a breadboard with jumper-wire sensor connections — is not constructed to the mechanical, electromagnetic, or environmental durability requirements that EN 54 or UL product certification mandates. Achieving certification would require, at minimum: (a) migration of the circuit to a custom PCB with appropriate conformal coating and enclosure rated to the relevant IP class; (b) replacement of prototyping interconnects with solder-terminated or connector-locked sensor interfaces; (c) formalised software validation against EN 54-29 detection sensitivity test fires (TF1–TF5, TF8); and (d) EMC pre-compliance testing. These steps represent engineering development work beyond the scope of the current thesis but constitute a well-defined roadmap for a commercial or institutional deployment variant.
</localData>

The gap between prototype and certified product is a common challenge for academic IoT fire detection research. Pfeifer et al. (2025) highlight that IoT-based air quality and gas monitoring networks show strong functional alignment with fire detection requirements yet are explicitly positioned as supplementary systems rather than primary FDAS replacements, precisely because they lack the regulatory approval infrastructure that EN 54 certification confers. This framing is appropriate for the present system: the Autonomous Sensing Node is best characterised as a complementary edge intelligence layer capable of operating alongside — and eventually informing — certified FDAS infrastructure, rather than as a standalone replacement. ### Glossary

- **BOM (Bill of Materials):** An itemised list of all hardware components and their associated unit costs required to build one instance of the system.
- **ESP-NOW:** A connectionless, peer-to-peer WiFi communication protocol developed by Espressif for low-latency data transfer between ESP32 devices without router infrastructure.
- **FDAS (Fire Detection and Alarm System):** A complete system comprising detectors, control panels, notification appliances, and interconnecting wiring, assessed as a whole under EN 54 or NFPA 72.
- **Notified Body:** A conformity assessment organisation designated by an EU member state to evaluate products against harmonised European standards (e.g., EN 54) for CE marking purposes.
- **Static Location Tagging:** Assignment of a fixed `Zone_ID` string to each node's MQTT payload at firmware configuration time, providing room-level spatial context without GPS hardware.
- **UL (Underwriters Laboratories):** A US-based safety certification organisation; UL 268 (smoke detectors), UL 521 (heat detectors), and UL 864 (fire alarm control units) are the primary applicable standards.
- **Zone_ID:** A hardcoded string field embedded in each node's MQTT telemetry payload to identify its physical installation location (e.g., `Zone_ID: Kitchen_North`).
