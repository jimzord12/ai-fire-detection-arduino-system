# 4.1 Requirements for Multi-Modal Sensing

The effective early detection of fire in indoor environments demands a sensing architecture that moves beyond single-modality approaches. Single-sensor systems — whether relying solely on smoke, temperature, or gas concentration — are inherently susceptible to false activations and limited discrimination capability, because each physical phenomenon that a fire produces can also be generated by benign domestic or industrial activities (Khan et al., 2022). A well-designed fire detection node must therefore define its sensing requirements against a set of functional criteria: coverage of multiple fire-relevant physical phenomena, low false-alarm susceptibility, compatibility with low-power embedded hardware, and support for data-driven classification algorithms. This section establishes those requirements as the normative basis for the sensor selections detailed in Section 4.2.

## 4.1.1 Limitations of Single-Modality Detection and the Need for Multi-Modal Coverage

Early fire detection systems relied on a single temperature or smoke sensor employing a single threshold judgment; however, these systems are susceptible to interference and exhibit low detection accuracy (Deng et al., 2023). The core limitation is that no single physical fire signature is both necessary and sufficient to confirm the presence of combustion. Smoke particles may arise from cooking fumes or steam; elevated CO can result from vehicle exhaust or faulty gas appliances; infrared radiation is produced by incandescent lamps and heating elements. As a result, systems that monitor only one modality face an unavoidable precision–recall trade-off: thresholds low enough for early detection inevitably accept high false-alarm rates, while conservative thresholds delay or suppress valid alarms.

Multi-sensor synergistic detectors address this limitation by capturing concurrent changes across several fire-relevant variables (Li et al., 2022). During the early growth phase of a real fire, temperature rises, CO concentration increases as incomplete combustion products form, VOC species are released through pyrolysis, smoke particles scatter optical signals, and, once a flame is established, near-infrared radiance in the 760 nm–1100 nm band becomes detectable (Khan et al., 2022). No single nuisance source simultaneously activates all of these modalities, so requiring corroborated evidence across multiple channels is the fundamental mechanism by which false-alarm susceptibility is reduced. A multi-modal architecture therefore establishes an inter-sensor redundancy that increases overall system reliability without requiring an increase in per-sensor sensitivity.

## 4.1.2 Functional Sensing Requirements for the Target Node

Translating the multi-modal rationale into concrete design requirements for an edge-deployed node operating on an Arduino UNO R4 WiFi (Renesas RA4M1, 48 MHz Cortex-M4, 32 kB SRAM) yields five interconnected criteria.

**R1 — Chemical combustion marker detection.** The node must detect at least two independent chemical by-products of combustion: smoke (particulate matter) and CO. CO is identified by Khan et al. (2022) as the most reliable early-warning gas across all major fire types — including flaming, smoldering, and liquid-fuel fires — because it is produced in all six principal combustion classes studied in NIST experimental data. Smoke detection provides corroborating particulate evidence. Incorporating VOC sensing adds a third chemical channel sensitive to pyrolysis gases released prior to visible ignition, which is particularly important for smoldering scenarios where CO generation may lag behind the initial thermal decomposition of organic materials.

**R2 — Optical flame confirmation.** A dedicated near-infrared (IR) flame sensor covering the 760 nm–1100 nm spectral band must be included to provide direct radiant confirmation of a flaming combustion event. This modality is orthogonal to chemical sensing: cooking, steam, and VOC emissions do not produce the characteristic IR flicker signature of an open flame. The inclusion of an optical channel therefore directly supports the _false_alarm_ class discrimination objective central to the three-class classification architecture (Li et al., 2022).

**R3 — Thermal and humidity context.** Temperature and relative humidity readings from an integrated environmental sensor (AHT20) are required to provide ambient context for normalizing gas sensor output, because MEMS metal-oxide semiconductor (MOS) gas sensors exhibit resistance responses that are temperature-dependent (Khan et al., 2022). Additionally, elevated temperature and reduced humidity are themselves early fire indicators when interpreted jointly with gas and optical data; temperature-based feature fusion has been demonstrated to improve classification accuracy by over 2.5% relative to algorithms that exclude thermal context (Li et al., 2022).

**R4 — Compatibility with resource-constrained inference.** Each sensor must output an analog or low-bandwidth digital signal adequate for sampling at 10 Hz per channel. Deng et al. (2023) demonstrated that a fused feature vector drawn from smoke, CO, and temperature sampled within a 10 s sliding window (100 data points) is sufficient to achieve 99.1% classification accuracy on an embedded CNN, confirming that meaningful fire/no-fire discrimination is achievable within the memory and computational budget of a Cortex-M4-class microcontroller. The sensing front-end of the present node must therefore avoid high-bandwidth modalities (e.g., cameras or acoustic arrays) that would exceed available RAM and inference bandwidth.

**R5 — Physical co-location and low footprint.** All sensors must be integrable on a single compact node in order to ensure that the sensed variables describe the same local air parcel. Spatial separation between, for example, a smoke sensor and a temperature sensor introduces temporal decorrelation that degrades feature coherence in time-series classification (Li et al., 2022). MEMS technology satisfies this requirement by enabling millimeter-scale sensor elements that can be mounted within centimetres of one another on a single PCB, while consuming power compatible with USB or battery operation (Khan et al., 2022).

## 4.1.3 Three-Class Classification as a Driver of Sensor Selection

The three-class target taxonomy — _fire_, _no_fire_, and _false_alarm_ — imposes additional sensor selection constraints beyond those required for binary detection. Distinguishing _false_alarm_ events (cooking smoke, ethanol vapour, intense incandescent IR sources) from genuine _fire_ events requires sensors with sufficient chemical selectivity to resolve differences in gas composition between combustion and non-combustion aerosol sources. Li et al. (2022) demonstrated that the combination of smoke, CO, and temperature channels provides the minimum feature set required for reliable three-class discrimination in indoor scenarios; the addition of a VOC channel and an IR flame sensor extends this discriminability to scenarios involving high-VOC, low-CO nuisance sources such as cleaning solvents and cooking fumes. Deng et al. (2023) further showed that heterogeneous sensor combinations — particularly those pairing chemical with thermal channels — outperform homogeneous single-modality arrays because the inter-channel temporal relationships encode class-discriminative information that no single sensor can provide. The sensor suite selected for this work is therefore not an ad hoc assembly but follows directly from the multi-modal requirements defined above, each modality being justified by its specific contribution to reducing class confusion within the three-class framework.

## Glossary

- **MEMS (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems):** Miniaturized sensor elements fabricated through microfabrication techniques, combining mechanical and electronic components on a single chip substrate.
- **MOS (Metal-Oxide Semiconductor) gas sensor:** A chemoresistive sensor whose electrical resistance changes upon exposure to target gas molecules through surface adsorption and catalytic reaction.
- **VOC (Volatile Organic Compounds):** Organic chemicals with high vapour pressure at room temperature, released during pyrolysis and early-stage combustion prior to visible ignition.
- **Cortex-M4:** A 32-bit RISC ARM processor core with DSP and optional FPU extensions, designed for low-cost, energy-efficient real-time embedded applications.
- **False alarm (nuisance alarm):** An erroneous fire alarm activation triggered by a non-fire stimulus such as cooking fumes, steam, or ambient aerosols.
