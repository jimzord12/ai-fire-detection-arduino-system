# 4.3 Justification of Sensor Choice

The selection of sensors for a multi-modal fire detection node is governed by a fundamental engineering tension: sensitivity—the ability to detect a target analyte at low concentrations—and selectivity—the ability to respond exclusively to that analyte while rejecting interferents. For life-critical applications, neither property alone is sufficient. A highly sensitive but non-selective sensor generates excessive false alarms, while a highly selective but insensitive sensor fails to detect incipient fires (Fonollosa et al., 2018). The five sensors integrated in this node—the Fermion MEMS Smoke sensor, the Fermion VOC Gas sensor (MEMS), the Fermion MEMS CO sensor, the Gravity Analog IR Flame sensor, and the Fermion AHT20 Temperature and Humidity sensor—were chosen because their complementary selectivity profiles form a mutually reinforcing ensemble that resolves individual sensor limitations through fusion.

## 4.3.1 Sensitivity vs. Selectivity

The sensitivity–selectivity trade-off is especially acute in metal-oxide semiconductor (MOS) and MEMS-based gas sensors. MOS sensors exhibit high sensitivity to combustion gases at low cost and small form factor; however, their broad chemiresistive response means that a single sensor reacts to many structurally similar analytes, a phenomenon known as cross-sensitivity (Khan et al., 2022). For instance, a smoke sensor designed to respond to combustion aerosols may equally respond to cooking steam or airborne dust, which are dominant sources of false alarms in residential environments (Fonollosa et al., 2018). This cross-sensitivity problem has been empirically confirmed at the system level: Chagger and Glockling (2018) demonstrated in standardised EN 54-compliant test environments that single-technology optical smoke detectors triggered significantly more often during false alarm scenarios involving cooking aerosols, dust, and steam compared to multi-sensor systems, which cross-validated thermal, optical, and CO signals to delay or suppress those responses.

The integration of a dedicated CO sensor addresses a fundamental gap in smoke-only detection. CO appears in both smoldering and flaming fires across all standard fuel types, making its detection a high-confidence indicator of actual combustion rather than mere particle scattering (Fonollosa et al., 2018). Critically, common nuisance sources such as cooking aerosols, water vapor, and cleaning product fumes generate elevated smoke and VOC signals without a corresponding CO concentration increase. The CO sensor therefore acts as a chemical "truth gate": its positive response in conjunction with elevated smoke or VOC readings sharply narrows the probability of a false alarm classification, directly supporting the three-class (fire / no_fire / false_alarm) decision architecture of this node. This combustion-specific discriminant role is consistent with findings by Fonollosa et al. (2018), who observed that CO-equipped detectors triggered alarms for all tested smoldering fires while standalone photoelectric detectors failed to alarm in three of nine experiments.

The VOC sensor extends coverage to the pyrolysis phase that precedes visible smoke. Many synthetic polymers present in modern building interiors—upholstery, cables, foam, and plastics—emit volatile organic compounds during thermal decomposition before full combustion and before smoke particles are visible (Fonollosa et al., 2018). A VOC sensor therefore provides an earlier warning signal than a smoke sensor in smoldering scenarios. However, the same sensor exhibits sensitivity to ethanol-based cleaning products, perfumes, and cooking solvents—precisely the nuisance sources targeted by the false_alarm class. This characteristic cross-sensitivity is not a disqualifying deficiency but a design-exploitable property: when the VOC sensor activates without a concurrent CO elevation, the pattern is probabilistically consistent with a false_alarm event rather than fire, and the classifier is trained on exactly this distinguishing feature. This strategy aligns with the multi-criteria approach advocated by Fonollosa et al. (2018), wherein dynamic features and multivariate models that exploit sensor correlations are considered imperative for reliable chemical-based fire detection.

The IR Flame sensor (760 nm–1100 nm) provides a complementary modality that is physically orthogonal to gas-phase sensing. Open-flame combustion produces characteristic near-infrared radiation in this spectral band, which is absent in steam, dust, or VOC-emitting nuisance events (Khan et al., 2022). Its high specificity to radiant flame events—including the characteristic 10–15 Hz flicker of real flames—means that a positive IR signal provides strong evidence of actual fire and, when fused with gas sensor readings, substantially reduces the risk of misclassification. Conversely, the IR sensor's primary limitation—susceptibility to intense artificial IR sources such as incandescent lamps or direct sunlight—is mitigated architecturally by requiring concurrent gas-phase confirmation before a fire alarm is issued.

Temperature and humidity data from the AHT20 sensor serve a dual purpose in the selectivity framework. First, elevated ambient temperature is correlated with fire scenarios; however, cooking and HVAC events also raise local temperature transiently, rendering temperature alone an unreliable discriminant without supporting evidence from gas sensors (Chagger & Glockling, 2018). Second, humidity is a confounding variable for MOS and MEMS gas sensors: water vapor adsorbs onto metal-oxide sensing surfaces and alters baseline resistance, which can shift apparent gas concentration readings (Fonollosa et al., 2018). The AHT20 provides real-time humidity and temperature measurements (±2% RH, ±0.3 °C accuracy) that enable compensation of these environmental drifts during feature engineering, improving the effective selectivity of the gas sensor array without requiring hardware-level modifications to the sensors themselves.

Taken together, the five-sensor ensemble was selected because no individual sensor possesses sufficient selectivity to independently distinguish fire from false_alarm scenarios in residential and light-industrial indoor environments. The smoke sensor provides sensitivity to combustion particulates; the VOC sensor captures early pyrolysis products and false-alarm-triggering organic vapors; the CO sensor acts as a combustion-specific discriminant; the IR flame sensor confirms radiant open-flame events; and the AHT20 contextualizes and compensates measurements environmentally. This complementarity is the central justification for the chosen sensor suite: each sensor's cross-sensitivity profile, rather than being a liability, becomes a discriminative feature when interpreted jointly with the others through the multi-class TinyML classifier (Khan et al., 2022).

### Glossary

- **Sensitivity**: The minimum concentration of a target analyte that produces a measurable change in sensor output signal.
- **Selectivity**: The degree to which a sensor responds preferentially to its target analyte in the presence of interfering substances.
- **Cross-sensitivity**: The unintended response of a sensor to analytes other than its designated target, a primary driver of false alarms in single-sensor fire detectors.
- **Pyrolysis**: Thermochemical decomposition of organic material at elevated temperature in the absence of sufficient oxygen, producing volatile organic compounds that precede visible smoke.
- **Truth gate**: An informal term for a sensor whose signal must be co-present with other signals to confirm a classification; used here to describe the CO sensor's role in validating combustion events.
