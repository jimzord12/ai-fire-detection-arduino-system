# 4.4 Sensor Calibration Protocols and Baseline Behavior

This section addresses one of the most operationally critical aspects of multi-sensor fire detection: the design and execution of calibration protocols and the characterization of baseline behavior for each sensor deployed in the system. Because the classification performance of any machine learning model is directly bounded by the quality of its input signals, raw sensor outputs must be conditioned through systematic calibration before they can be used as training or inference data. The sensors in this node—comprising the MEMS smoke (ethanol-compatible), VOC, and CO gas sensors, the IR flame detector, and the AHT20 temperature/humidity transducer—each exhibit distinct warm-up transients, drift profiles, humidity dependencies, and cross-sensitivity behaviors that must be understood and mitigated through deliberate protocol design (Solórzano et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022).

## 4.4.1 Warm-Up and Thermal Stabilization

Metal oxide semiconductor (MOS) and MEMS gas sensors require a dedicated power-on warm-up period before their output stabilizes sufficiently for reliable measurement. During cold start, the resistive sensing element and its integrated micro-hotplate begin heating from ambient temperature to the target operating temperature, typically in the range of 250 °C to 450 °C for MOX-type devices (Solórzano et al., 2022). Until thermal equilibrium is reached, the sensor's output resistance drifts rapidly, producing signals that do not accurately reflect ambient gas concentrations. In the context of the present system, a mandatory warm-up delay must be enforced in firmware before any data sampling begins, or before the Edge Impulse inference window is populated.

For MOS-based smoke and VOC sensors, studies on multi-sensor fire detection platforms have operated MOX sensors at thermally controlled heater temperatures of 259 °C and 446 °C using pulse-width modulation (PWM) at 1 kHz, noting that stabilization of the sensor output requires a finite settling period following power application (Solórzano et al., 2022). MEMS gas sensor arrays specifically are sensitive to supply voltage deviations and ambient temperature fluctuations during the transient warm-up phase, and these disturbances are catalogued as _bias faults_ or _impulse faults_ in the sensor output—both of which are distinguishable from genuine gas responses through statistical monitoring (Liu et al., 2022). The firmware diagnostic routine (`verify_all_sensors_operational.ino`) in this project therefore gates data acquisition behind a configurable thermal stabilization interval, ensuring that readings are only accepted once output variance falls below a defined threshold.

For the AHT20 temperature and humidity sensor, no resistive heater is employed, and the device achieves stable digital output within approximately 10 s of power-on, given its capacitive MEMS humidity element and calibrated ASIC; its long-term drift is specified at less than 0.5%RH per year and less than 0.04 °C per year, making it the most inherently stable sensor in the array (ASAIR, 2020). The IR flame sensor, being a phototransistor sensitive to the 760 nm–1100 nm band, has no thermal warm-up requirement, but its output baseline shifts with ambient illumination and must be characterized in the expected deployment environment.

## 4.4.2 Baseline Establishment and Fresh-Air Reference

Establishing a reproducible _baseline_—the sensor output in the absence of any target gas or fire stimulus—is a prerequisite for computing meaningful differential responses and for training discriminative classification models. The canonical procedure consists of two stages: (1) exposing the sensor array to a known clean-air environment and recording the stabilized output as the zero reference, and (2) characterizing any temperature- and humidity-dependent variation of this reference across the expected environmental operating envelope (Liu et al., 2022).

In multi-sensor fire detection research, baseline acquisition protocols have been formally defined as the initial stage of each experimental run: the test environment is allowed to reach ambient equilibrium with no combustion materials or volatiles present, and sensor signals are recorded for a sufficient pre-stimulus window to establish the resting response (Solórzano et al., 2022). This baseline window serves a dual purpose: it provides the normalization anchor for differential features used in machine learning, and it exposes any inter-run drift that may have occurred since the previous calibration campaign. The present system implements this by recording a quiet idle period at the beginning of each data collection session, from which per-channel mean values are computed and stored as rolling baselines in firmware.

Humidity is a known confound for MEMS gas sensors: elevated relative humidity reduces the oxygen availability on the MOX sensing surface, directly shifting the baseline resistance and suppressing sensitivity to combustion gases (Liu et al., 2022). The simultaneous acquisition of temperature and relative humidity via the AHT20 enables humidity-aware baseline correction, a strategy employed in gas sensor array systems for fire detection to maintain calibration model validity across environmental conditions (Solórzano et al., 2022). Without this correction, a humid kitchen environment and a dry office environment would produce systematically different baselines for the same VOC and CO sensors, impairing the generalization of the trained classifier.

## 4.4.3 Sensor Drift Characterization and Compensation

Sensor drift—the gradual, time-dependent shift of the output baseline under constant stimulus conditions—represents one of the most significant long-term reliability challenges for MEMS gas sensor arrays deployed in fire detection applications (Liu et al., 2022). Drift arises from multiple concurrent mechanisms: irreversible oxidation and aging of the metal oxide sensing film, deposition of environmental contaminants on the sensing surface, changes in electrode contact resistance, and thermal cycling fatigue of the MEMS micro-hotplate structure. The net effect is that a calibration model trained at deployment time becomes progressively less predictive over months of operation (Solórzano et al., 2022).

In a controlled validation study spanning fifteen months, Solórzano et al. (2022) demonstrated that a PLS-DA model trained on gas sensor array data retained 100% specificity but experienced a reduction in sensitivity from near-perfect to 85% when evaluated four months post-calibration, particularly for plastic fire signatures whose spectral profile migrated toward nuisance scenarios under sensor drift. This quantitative finding directly motivates the inclusion of a drift compensation mechanism in the present firmware. Liu et al. (2022) proposed a lightweight Self-Detection and Self-Calibration (SDSC) strategy for MEMS gas sensor arrays, specifically designed for deployment on resource-constrained IoT microcontrollers, which operates via three sequential stages: (1) online fault detection using a Principal Component Analysis–Squared Prediction Error (PCA-SPE) statistical model, (2) single and multiple fault isolation and recovery using data reconstruction and bootstrap-based confidence interval prediction, and (3) dynamic baseline differential processing for real-time drift compensation. The PCA-SPE detection model achieved an average fault detection computation time of 0.28 ms and consumed 9.884 mW on a CC1350 microcontroller, confirming its viability for deployment on a Cortex-M4 platform such as the Renesas RA4M1 used in this work (Liu et al., 2022).

The drift compensation approach adopted for this system implements a dynamic baseline matrix, updated continuously from periods of confirmed clean-air exposure, against which current sensor outputs are differenced. This technique approximates the automatic baseline calibration (ABC) paradigm, which leverages low-stimulus intervals to autonomously reset the sensor zero point and neutralize both short-term environmental shifts and long-term aging drift (Liu et al., 2022). For the CO sensor specifically—identified in this project as the _truth sensor_ for combustion verification—drift compensation is especially important, as even modest baseline offsets can induce false positives or mask genuine CO elevation from smoldering fires.

## 4.4.4 Cross-Sensitivity and Inter-Sensor Consistency Verification

Cross-sensitivity—the response of a sensor to chemical species other than its primary target—is an intrinsic property of MOX and MEMS gas sensors that directly affects the reliability of fire versus false-alarm discrimination. The MEMS smoke sensor responds to ethanol and other VOCs in addition to combustion aerosols; the VOC sensor responds non-selectively across a range of organic vapors including cooking fumes, cleaning solvents, and alcohol; and the CO sensor may exhibit cross-sensitivity to hydrogen and certain hydrocarbons (Solórzano et al., 2022). In fire detection, these cross-sensitivities are the root cause of the false-alarm problem, as cooking, cleaning, and industrial activities generate volatile profiles that partially overlap with fire signatures.

The calibration protocol for this system therefore includes deliberate _cross-sensitivity characterization runs_, in which known nuisance stimuli—ethanol vapor, cooking aerosols, steam—are introduced individually and the response of each sensor is recorded and labeled. This procedure directly mirrors the nuisance scenario methodology described by Solórzano et al. (2022), where a portfolio of nuisances including gasoline, vinegar, ethanol, turpentine, air freshener, and window cleaner was systematically tested in an EN 54–compliant standard fire room and a small-scale validation chamber. The resulting cross-sensitivity profiles inform the feature engineering and class boundary design of the three-class classifier (fire / no_fire / false_alarm), ensuring that the sensor array's known ambiguities are explicitly represented in the training dataset rather than treated as noise.

Operationally, inter-sensor consistency is verified using the `verify_all_sensors_operational.ino` diagnostic firmware, which at power-on performs a self-check confirming that: (a) each analog channel is producing a non-saturated, non-zero output within the expected quiescent range; (b) the AHT20 responds correctly to its I²C address and returns values within the specified operating range of −40 °C to +85 °C and 0%RH to 100%RH; and (c) the IR flame sensor output remains within its dark-ambient baseline level, confirming the absence of IR flooding from sunlight or artificial incandescent sources (ASAIR, 2020). Any sensor failing this consistency check triggers a fault flag that inhibits inference and raises an alert, preventing degraded sensor data from contaminating the training or operational classification pipeline—an approach aligned with the fault detection philosophy formalized by Liu et al. (2022).

## Glossary

- **Baseline**: The quiescent output of a sensor in the absence of any target stimulus, used as the zero-reference for differential response computation.
- **Cross-Sensitivity**: The response of a sensor to chemical species other than its intended target analyte.
- **Drift**: A slow, time-dependent shift in the sensor's baseline output unrelated to changes in the target gas concentration, typically caused by material aging or environmental contamination.
- **MOX sensor**: Metal oxide semiconductor gas sensor; operates by measuring changes in surface resistance upon adsorption of target gases onto a heated metal oxide film.
- **PCA-SPE**: Principal Component Analysis–Squared Prediction Error; a multivariate statistical method used for real-time fault detection in sensor arrays by monitoring residual variance in the PCA residual subspace.
- **PWM**: Pulse-Width Modulation; a technique used to control heater power in MEMS gas sensors by varying the duty cycle of the applied voltage signal.
